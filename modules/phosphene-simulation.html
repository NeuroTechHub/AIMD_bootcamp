Phosphene Simulator

A live, no-Python playground for the same equations as dynaphos. Move the sliders, watch the basis change. Use this to build intuition before (or alongside) the notebook.

What is dynaphos? An open, differentiable forward model of phosphene perception under cortical microstimulation (van der Grinten et al., eLife 2024). It maps a current per electrode all the way to what the user sees, through a short pipeline you'll meet section by section: current (µA per electrode) → effective current = current above rheobase (the per-pulse threshold below which no spikes are evoked) → charge per second (effective current × pulse width × frequency) → activation (a running low-pass integral of charge per second, §04) → trace (a slower reservoir that subtracts from drive — the model of neural fatigue, §04) → per-phosphene brightness (mapped from current activation) and shape (a Gaussian whose σ comes from current spread K and the eccentricity-dependent cortical magnification M(r)). 01One phosphene

A single electrode evokes a Gaussian-shaped bright spot. Its position is set by the retinotopic map; its size is set by current and local cortical magnification.

The whole module in one sentence. Every percept on this page is a sum of Gaussians, one per electrode. Their positions follow cortical magnification (dipole model). Their sizes follow current spread. Adjust the sliders below and watch which equations bite.

Slide eccentricity from 0.5 to 14. What happens to the phosphene?

It grows. Cortical magnification M(r) drops with eccentricity, and sigma_deg = radius_to_sigma * sqrt(I/K) / M(r). Smaller M -> bigger apparent size for the same current. This is why peripheral phosphenes appear large and blob-like, while foveal ones are crisp dots.

Halve K. Does the phosphene get bigger or smaller? Why?

Bigger. K is the current-spread constant (uA/mm^2). A smaller K means the same current spreads over a larger cortical area, activating more tissue -> larger phosphene radius -> larger sigma.

02Phosphene population

Real prostheses use hundreds to thousands of electrodes. The placement strategy determines what kinds of percepts are achievable.

The placement controls here are abstract — real arrays aren't. On the page you pick "foveated vs uniform" by sampling probabilistically. In a real implant the placement is constrained by anatomy and surgery: V1 is mostly buried in the calcarine sulcus, so only a fraction of the cortical surface is reachable without entering tissue; vasculature must be avoided; arrays come in fixed geometries (the Utah array is a 4×4 mm grid of 96 shanks, 400 µm pitch); electrode pitch can't shrink indefinitely without crosstalk and tissue damage; and V1's cortical magnification means foveal eccentricities consume far more cortex than peripheral ones, so a fixed array footprint covers different visual-field eccentricity ranges depending on where it's placed. Today's research and early-clinical cortical prostheses (e.g. Orion / Second Sight — now ended; Cortigent; the ICVP / CORTIVIS efforts; Neuralink B1) operate within these constraints.

Switch from foveated to uniform. Which gives finer central detail?

Foveated. With the same N electrodes, foveated placement crowds them near the fovea, where sigma is also smaller, producing crisper central vision. Uniform placement gives the periphery the same density as the fovea but each phosphene is small in the centre and large in the periphery, wasting resolution on the easy regions and falling apart in the periphery anyway.

Set N = 200. Roughly how small can a feature be before you can't make it out?

The visual field is 16 deg wide (8 deg radius, ≈ 200 deg2 of area). Spreading 200 electrodes uniformly gives one electrode per ≈ 1 deg2, so the typical inter-electrode spacing is on the order of 1 deg. Two phosphenes start to merge below about 3σ separation, so features finer than a few times the typical sigma (also ∼ 0.5–1 deg here) won't be resolvable. In Snellen terms (the chart-distance notation for visual acuity, 20/x = "I read at 20 ft what a sighted user reads at x ft") that lands in the ballpark of 20/600 — broadly the legal-blindness range — though the exact number depends on layout and sigma. The point is that even with hundreds of electrodes, prosthetic vision is acuity-limited.

Pick the grating. How fine a grating can you still resolve?

The limit is the Nyquist of the electrode lattice in the relevant region. Bars finer than ~2x the inter-electrode spacing alias to a uniform field (or a coarser bar pattern). This is the "phosphene acuity" upper bound.

Walk peak current down: 20, then 25, then 35–40 µA. What happens at each step?

Two thresholds gate the percept and they're easy to confuse:

  • 20 µA: below dynaphos's rheobase of 23.9 µA. Every pixel that maps to less than rheobase produces zero charge per second — the electrodes go silent and the canvas goes black.
  • 25 µA: just above rheobase, so charge per second is positive but tiny. The leaky activation integrator (§04) needs time and head-room above rheobase to climb past the brightness threshold; with only the brightest pixels barely above it, you'll see at most a faint speckle, not a coherent image.
  • 35–40 µA: comfortably above rheobase. Brightness emerges first — you can see the image is there before you can tell what shape it has. As you climb further, the Gaussian shape resolves last. Shape is more robust than brightness because shape depends on which electrodes fire, not how much each is firing.

04Temporal dynamics

Each electrode is a leaky integrator. Watch brightness build up under sustained stim and decay after it stops.

Vocabulary for this section. The dynaphos pipeline introduced at the top of the page bottlenecks through two state variables that this playground exposes — they're the source of every behaviour you'll see below. charge_per_s = max(I − rheobase, 0) × pulse_width × frequency. The driving signal: how much net charge the electrode delivers per second, after subtracting the rheobase threshold. Zero below rheobase, linear above it. activation = a running low-pass integral of charge_per_s. Mathematically this is a leaky integrator — a one-pole filter, dx/dt = input − activation_decay · x — that accumulates input and forgets it exponentially. activation_decay (units: s−1) is the inverse time constant: large values forget fast, small values integrate over many seconds. Brightness is read out from activation, not from the instantaneous current. trace = a much slower second reservoir that's added to rheobase as an extra leak. As trace builds up under sustained stim, less of your current ends up as effective charge, so charge_per_s shrinks, activation drops, brightness fades. That is what we call adaptation — the loss of percept under sustained stim that real cortex shows over seconds (Fernández et al., 2021). The "disable trace" mode kills this term and turns the cortex into a perfect transducer.

With the yaml default (1.2e-4), why does brightness drop during sustained stim?

This is adaptation, not decay. Dynaphos has two states: fast activation (which tracks instantaneous charge) and slow trace (which accumulates). The trace adds to the leak current, so the effective amplitude (I - rheobase - trace) shrinks over ~1 second, charge drops, activation drops, brightness fades. Switch to "disable trace" to see what happens without it.

Compare "state preserved across frames" against "no adaptation". Which behaviour matches what cortical neurons actually do under sustained stim?

"State preserved across frames" runs both activation and trace; the trace builds up under sustained stim and the percept fades over seconds. "No adaptation" keeps the activation integrator but kills the trace, so the percept stays bright indefinitely — what you'd see if cortex were a perfect transducer. Real cortical neurons adapt (Fernández et al., 2021, NEJM): sustained stim produces a percept that decays over seconds. Dynaphos's defaults are calibrated to that human implant data, so the "state preserved" mode is the realistic one.

Crank activation_decay/s to 0.5. What happens?

You override the yaml. Activation now decays exp(-0.69/35) per frame ≈ 2% per frame instead of essentially-instantly. This is the same as making the activation integrator slow. Brightness now builds over many frames before saturating - the dynaphos default treats activation as effectively instant.

05Self-check

Without running anything, predict the answer first; then check with the sliders above.

Q1. Doubling the current with K and radius_to_sigma fixed: how much does sigma grow?

sigma scales as sqrt(I/K), so doubling I scales sigma by sqrt(2) ~ 1.41x.

Q2. Where is the cortical-magnification factor M largest?

Near the fovea (small r). M(r) = k * (1/(r+a) - 1/(r+b)) in the dipole model; both fractions decrease in r, but the first faster, so M is monotonically decreasing.

Q3. You have 1000 electrodes. To read text, what layout?

Foveated, low radius_to_sigma. Text needs small features near where the eye is fixating, which is the foveal pit. Don't waste electrodes on the far periphery.

Q4. Rheobase is 23.9 µA. What does setting stim to 24 µA do?

Almost nothing. charge_per_s = (I - rheobase) × pw × freq with I - rheobase = 0.1 µA gives an essentially zero effective current. Below the activation threshold (9.14e-8) you get zero brightness. Useful current range starts around 30 µA and saturates near 100 µA.
